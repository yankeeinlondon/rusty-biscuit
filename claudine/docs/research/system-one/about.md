# System One decision models: research and open alternatives

**Research checked:** September 28, 2026. The project landscape changes quickly; repository links lead to the primary project pages. Metrics below are author-reported unless explicitly identified as an independent benchmark. Results from different datasets and hardware are not directly comparable.

## What this category means

TypeSafe describes Jev and “System One models” as models for making fast, typed decisions that software consumes directly. A request supplies a state and typed questions; the response supplies a choice, score, or yes/no probability. In contrast, a conventional autoregressive LLM is trained and served to predict/generate a sequence of text tokens. It can express a decision in prose or structured output, but usually emits tokens, and an application must constrain or parse the response. Jev’s framing removes free-form generation from this decision path: candidate outputs are fixed by the caller and the model scores them in parallel. TypeSafe also says Jev is trained with Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD), and claims 70–500 ms end-to-end latency and 40–200× speedups on its selected decision tasks. These are vendor claims, not a universal comparison. [TypeSafe announcement](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev)

The important distinction is therefore **task and interface**, not a demonstrated wholly new mathematical species. Classification, ranking, discriminative encoders, probabilistic prediction, selective prediction, and calibration have long academic histories. A classifier can score a caller-supplied label set in one forward pass; it does not need to generate explanatory language. A System One-style model generalizes that interface to runtime-defined questions and answer types, aiming to reuse one model across changing decision schemas. It can still use transformer encoders or LLM backbones. Several community implementations simply inspect a generative model’s next-token logits, and others fine-tune an encoder or add a decision head.

The relevant research foundation is mature:

- [Guo et al., “On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks” (ICML 2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599) defines calibration as probabilities reflecting empirical correctness and shows that accuracy does not guarantee calibrated confidence. Temperature scaling can improve calibration on held-out data, but a softmax distribution or a confidence field alone is not evidence of calibration.
- [Geifman and El-Yaniv, “Selective Classification for Deep Neural Networks” (NeurIPS 2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500) studies abstaining on uncertain inputs and trading coverage for lower risk. This is the established basis for routing uncertain decisions to a human or slower model.
- [Lakshminarayanan et al., “Simple and Scalable Predictive Uncertainty Estimation using Deep Ensembles” (NeurIPS 2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474) demonstrates uncertainty estimates from ensembles and highlights evaluation under distribution shift. Calibration measured on an in-domain validation split does not establish reliable uncertainty on new domains.

These papers support the components (classification, calibration, abstention, uncertainty estimation), but do not establish “System One model” as a new academic taxonomy. I found no peer-reviewed paper defining Jev’s claimed model class or independently reproducing its RLCD training. TypeSafe has not published Jev’s weights, parameter count, full architecture, or sufficient RLCD detail to reproduce it. Its “cannot hallucinate” claim should be read narrowly: a finite typed answer set prevents arbitrary free-form output, but does not prevent a wrong answer, bad confidence estimate, or an invalid decision caused by incomplete state.

For credible evaluation, compare task accuracy and throughput under the same prompts, batch size, hardware, and serving stack, and report calibration (ECE and Brier score), coverage/risk when abstaining, and out-of-distribution results. Published alternative metrics below mostly come from each project’s own task suite; there is not yet a stable common benchmark. Latency figures also vary between model-only and end-to-end measurements.

## Open-source and open-weight alternatives found

“Alternative” covers several different things: a trained model intended for decisions, a method for extracting candidate scores from an existing model, or a runtime/API wrapper. An API-compatible clone does not reproduce Jev’s undisclosed training. “Open source” below refers to published code; weights and datasets may have separate terms. Check each linked repository/model card before deployment.

### Additional projects and adjacent substitutes

The following smaller or less directly comparable projects also appeared in the live directory scan. Their own pages provide limited evidence, so I record the published architecture and avoid inferring performance where no reproducible figure was available.

- **Tev1** — Qwen3.5-4B LoRA fine-tune retaining the usual next-token language-model head; Together AI serves it as an option-letter choice API. It answers Choice only, and the authors warn that log-probabilities are not calibrated confidence. Their 880/1,000 result is on a reused development set, not an independent evaluation. [Together AI/Tev1](https://github.com/togethercomputer/tev1)
- **Bespoke Nimble** — Qwen3.5-9B LoRA-tuned decision model; author-reported 90.12% against Jev’s 93.21% on 324 held-out examples. [Bespoke/Nimble](https://huggingface.co/bespoke-studio/nimble)
- **Rizzo Flow** — frozen Spark-X2.5 4B/1.7B served via llama.cpp; reported tied with SemIf on SemIf’s own test set. [Rizzo Flow](https://github.com/rizzoflow/rizzo-flow)
- **GLiNER2.5-Decide** — DeBERTa-v3-large-based GLiNER encoder fine-tuned for decision questions, extraction, relations, and structured records. Fastino reports 60.1% over its 17-dataset suite versus 57.5% for its JevK5 baseline; p50 167 ms on a 48-vCPU CPU and 38–47 ms on GPU. These are vendor’s own results; the model card’s parameter count (340M) differs from Hugging Face weight metadata (486M). [Fastino Labs/GLiNER2.5-Decide](https://huggingface.co/fastino/gliner2.5-decide)
- **Tev1 / Nimble alternatives** have hosted or downloadable components; verify the weights’ separate license metadata before assuming the code license covers model weights.
- **litjev** — Qwen-backed implementation of Choice, Score, and Noul with Jev-compatible schema and an MMLU-Pro benchmark; no comparable result found in the source summary. [zhengxuyu/litjev](https://github.com/zhengxuyu/litjev)
- **Reflex** — Qwen3.5-4B GPU reimplementation, batching fixed-option questions and returning option scores. No standard comparison located. [kshetrajna12/reflex](https://github.com/kshetrajna12/reflex)
- **Typical** — reads decision scores from a language model in one forward pass, using candidate-score statistics and a null/abstain value; its research page reports ECE 0.04 for one variant. [Typical research](https://typical.ozlabs.ai/research)
- **Nano-Laya** — multilingual, non-autoregressive decision engine with a language-based checkpoint router; temperature metadata is applied at checkpoint load. No common benchmark located. [yanfeng98/nano-laya](https://github.com/yanfeng98/nano-laya)
- **open-spark-Jev** — Qwen3-based local decision models aimed at NVIDIA DGX Spark; no standardized accuracy or speed results found. [abhishek085/open-spark-jev](https://github.com/abhishek085/open-spark-jev)
- **Parallel Constrained Decoding (Qwen2.5-1B-RLCD)** — a stock quantized Qwen inference technique rather than an RLCD-trained model; see the table above for its reported 5.6–7.0× local latency reduction. [Hugging Face Space](https://huggingface.co/spaces/drinkmoonshine/parallel-constrained-decoding)
- **Sales-conversion RL model** — PPO policy over BAAI/bge-m3 sequence embeddings, emitting conversion probabilities without text generation. It is a task-specific precursor claim, not a general Jev replacement; the author’s architecture-priority claim is not independent validation. [Hugging Face project](https://huggingface.co/DeepMostInnovations/sales-conversion-model-reinf-learning) · [arXiv 2503.23303](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23303)
- **Open-source general-purpose classifiers** such as [SetFit](https://github.com/huggingface/setfit) (Sentence Transformer embeddings plus a lightweight supervised head), [GLiNER](https://github.com/urchade/GLiNER) (label-conditioned entity extraction), and [ModernBERT](https://github.com/AnswerDotAI/ModernBERT) can be cheaper and accurate for fixed taxonomies or extraction, but do not expose Jev’s general typed-decision contract by themselves.
- **Structured-output frameworks** such as [Outlines](https://github.com/dottxt-ai/outlines), [Instructor](https://github.com/567-labs/instructor), and [DSPy](https://github.com/stanfordnlp/dspy) constrain or validate generated LLM output. They improve schema compliance, not model latency or calibrated decision uncertainty.

The [System One Models alternatives index](https://systemonemodels.org/examples/alternatives/) tracks a longer tail of small, fast-moving experiments (including near-duplicate names and deployment wrappers). I excluded the named projects in the prompt—Kev, Laya, and CLM—and focused the table on independently identifiable code/model alternatives. The index also lists general-purpose structured-output frameworks and conventional classifiers; they are useful substitutes for some workloads but are not System One models, so they are not presented as direct model equivalents here.

## Reading the claims

The strongest recurring engineering idea is straightforward: avoid autoregressive output-token generation when the caller already knows the legal answer space. That can reduce latency and guarantee shape. It does not itself improve judgment quality or make model probabilities trustworthy. Some alternatives reuse ordinary LLM next-token probabilities, some train a decision head, and a few claim post-hoc or reward-based calibration. Their output distributions have different semantics and must be calibrated and evaluated for the target domain before confidence thresholds drive consequential actions.

A further limit is expressiveness: these systems select/score among supplied choices; they do not replace an LLM for drafting, explanation, open-ended synthesis, or multi-step reasoning. A practical architecture can route common bounded judgments to a small decision model and send low-confidence or open-ended cases to an LLM or human reviewer. That is an application design pattern, not evidence that “System One” is inherently safer or more reliable.
